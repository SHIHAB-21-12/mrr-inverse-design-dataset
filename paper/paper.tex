\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Whether a naive inverse network can design photonic devices is disputed: Liu
\emph{et al.}\ [3] reported it failing to train when training pairs conflict, while
Chen and Jiang [7] and Adibnia \emph{et al.}\ [9] reported it working. We show
that the answer can depend on the error criterion. On $1{,}150$ labelled geometries
of a bus-coupled add-drop silicon microring (200 three-dimensional FDTD coupler
simulations, coupled-mode ring), a plain inverse network \emph{fails} in geometry
space ($15.5\%$ RMS relative geometry error) yet \emph{passes} in response space:
re-simulated in FDTD, its designs reproduce the requested response to $3.99\%$ RMS (96 of 100 targets buildable).
Four targets impose in effect two independent constraints, so a two-dimensional
family of geometries gives the same response, and geometry error does not predict
response error. A differential-evolution optimiser on the analytic model reaches
$2.89\%$ closed-loop error with no training data, so the networks give no accuracy
advantage here. Posed as constraints rather than point targets, two
co-packaged-optics channel specifications are met by direct optimisation (Scenario B at model level only), and the Scenario A design passes a complete 3-D ring simulation. The ring
model's resonance uncertainty ($1.55$\,nm) exceeds the $\pm0.4$\,nm tolerance of the eight-channel case, so fabricated devices would still need trimming. Results are for
one design space and one analytic ring model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Inverse design asks for a geometry given a desired optical response. Where a
forward simulation is expensive, a natural approach is to train a surrogate on
simulated data and then invert it, and a substantial literature now does
exactly this for nanophotonic components [1,2,4]. Within that literature sits a
specific, unresolved disagreement about whether the simplest approach works.

Liu \emph{et al.}\ [3] argued that training a network to map response
directly to geometry fails when several geometries produce the same response:
conflicting training pairs make training hard to converge. In their 20-layer
thin-film example (500{,}000 training instances) the direct inverse network's
geometry loss barely fell in 15{,}000 epochs and the structure it designed was
far from the target spectrum. Their remedy, the tandem network, trains the
inverse network through a frozen forward model so that the loss is measured in
response space, where the relation is single-valued.

Two later studies report direct inverse networks working on other devices.
Chen and Jiang [7] trained a forward and an inverse network independently (no
tandem) for a four-ring channel-dropping filter (five geometric parameters,
100-point spectra, 25{,}000 two-dimensional FDTD samples); they report a small geometry-space test loss (not a relative error) and show six FDTD re-simulations that match their
targets qualitatively. Adibnia \emph{et al.}\ [9] did likewise for a plasmonic
ring-resonator switch (five geometric parameters, 800-point spectra, 147{,}456 simulated examples); they report training losses of order $10^{-4}$ (a mean squared logarithmic error, not comparable with the other studies' losses), acknowledge the many-to-one problem, and
show for a worst-case target that the predicted and true structures differ while
their spectra agree. The three studies are not directly comparable: devices,
parameter counts, spectral dimensions and training-set sizes all differ. The losses reported in [7] and [9] are not normalised as ours is and are not comparable with it, and [9] itself shows a case in which the predicted and true structures differ while their spectra agree. Whether the difference between [3] and [7,9] lies in the error criterion or in how redundant each design space is was not tested here. What none of the three provides
is a systematic response-space statistic over many held-out targets next to the
geometry-space error. We measure both on one device.

No prior study we are aware of tests this for a bus-coupled add-drop microring resonator; scoring designs by their re-simulated response is not new in itself (see below). Pal \emph{et al.}\ [6] apply decision-tree and random-forest regression to microresonator inverse design on 462 finite-element samples, and two recent preprints apply machine learning to microrings with different aims. Simsek \emph{et al.}\ [15] use neural networks to recover the width and height of a silicon-nitride ring from its dispersion data, a characterisation problem with no coupling section. Hasani \emph{et al.}\ [16] combine a generative network with gradient boosting on FDTD data for a microring parameterised by radius, gap and width, and report RMSE together with one FDTD-checked high-$Q$ design. The design space of Pal \emph{et al.}\ [6] (radius, width and height, with the integrated dispersion of an isolated ring as input) contains no bus-coupling parameter at all and so cannot predict insertion loss or any
other coupling-dependent quantity --- the specifications that decide whether a
filter is usable in a co-packaged-optics (CPO) link. Torabi \emph{et al.}\
[12], the most recent study and the closest in parameterisation, defines the $\rge$ metric adopted here but addresses a larger biosensor ring (target $R = \SI{200}{\micro\metre}$, $w = h = \SI{2}{\micro\metre}$, with a thickness parameter), compares its physics-informed graph network only with a diffusion model and an ablation of itself, and includes no classical-machine-learning baseline; it is also an unrefereed
preprint and is cited as such throughout. Valencia-Garz\'on \emph{et al.}\
[10] release a $4{,}719$-sample all-pass silicon-nitride dataset of scalar metrics without modelling it. Optimisation-based and hybrid approaches also exist: DeepAdjoint [5] combines a conditional generative network with adjoint optimisation for metasurfaces, and Sadeghi Dizaji and Habibiyan [8] apply Bayesian optimisation with knowledge constraints to microring design for a quantum light source; the direct-optimiser baseline of Section~\ref{sec:baseline} is of this second kind.

Methods that return several solutions, rather than one, are the usual remedy for non-uniqueness. Frising \emph{et al.}\ [18] use a conditional invertible network, tested on a metallic film with subwavelength indentations and compared with a conditional variational autoencoder, and Ren \emph{et al.}\ [17] benchmark eight methods (among them tandem, neural-adjoint, mixture-density, variational and invertible networks) on electromagnetic-material design problems, scoring each by re-simulation error and proposing a measure of how ill-posed a problem is. We implement none of the distribution-valued methods: our comparison is limited to a plain network, a tandem network, Random Forest, XGBoost and, in Section~\ref{sec:baseline}, a direct optimiser, and a benchmark that adds the distribution-valued methods is left to future work. Scoring an inverse design by the response of the re-simulated or reconstructed design, rather than by its geometry, is established: it is the basis of the benchmark in [17], whose measure of ill-posedness is a ratio of two re-simulation errors, and of the tandem-network study in [21]. What we add is the direct comparison with geometry error on a bus-coupled add-drop ring.

This paper contributes the following.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A test of the disagreement}, measured on one dataset with
one protocol: the naive inverse network fails in geometry space and succeeds in response space, so on this device the two criteria disagree; whether this explains the differing reports of [3] and [7,9] was not tested. We give a closed-form reason, by
counting how many independent constraints four scalar targets actually impose, and
show the resulting degeneracy directly in the data: pairs of geometries with
indistinguishable targets that differ by up to $45.8\%$.
\item \textbf{Evidence that geometry-space error is the wrong metric}, and a physically motivated replacement ($\theta$ error) that tracks response-space error across the four models that were re-simulated (a consistency check, not an independent test).
\item \textbf{A measurement of physical realisability as a first-class
result}, with a short argument that one model family (Random Forest) cannot violate the convex design box.
\item \textbf{A reproducible information-bound estimate} for this design space, intended to separate model limitations from information limits; its bootstrap interval over the test targets contains the $15\%$ threshold, so it supports no claim of reachability.
\item \textbf{A controlled four-way comparison} of Random Forest, XGBoost, a
plain deep network and a tandem network on an identical split with identical
metrics, including a sample-efficiency characterisation --- the comparison
absent from [4,6,7,9,12].
\item \textbf{A direct-optimisation baseline and an error budget:} differential
evolution on the analytic model, with no training data, matches the networks in
3-D FDTD closed loop, the remaining error is traced to the coupler model's
$\kappa^2$ scatter against FDTD, and the benefit of choosing among equivalent designs is small: about a fifth of the modelled resonance spread for width variation alone, and almost nothing once thickness variation of $1$\,nm or more is included (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}).
\item \textbf{A validation that reports its own gaps}: the closed-loop check
is extended to a hundred targets and its buildability failures are counted, and
a $2.5$--$3.6\%$ group-index disagreement between the analytic ring and
full 3-D FDTD is traced, with a straight-guide test, to the FDTD engine's
dispersionless material fit.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Device, design space and methods}

\subsection{Device and parameterisation}

The device is a two-bus, four-port add-drop racetrack microring resonator on
silicon-on-insulator: silicon core of fixed height \SI{220}{nm}, SiO$_2$
cladding, fundamental TE mode only, scanned over
\SIrange{1500}{1600}{nm} with a nominal target of \SI{1550}{nm}. Both
couplers share a gap and coupling length, keeping the design space at exactly
four dimensions. The mode solver uses dispersive (Palik) material data; the FDTD engine's fit of those data proved to be effectively dispersionless over the band (Appendix~\ref{sec:appring}). A racetrack rather than a circular ring makes the coupling
length a genuine continuous parameter, which is the degree of freedom absent
from [6], [10] and [12].

Table~\ref{tab:space} lists the four free parameters and the four targets. The
round-trip length is $L = 2\pi R + 2L_c$.

\subsection{Dual-fidelity dataset construction}

A full three-dimensional FDTD simulation of the complete ring was measured at
roughly \SI{35}{\hour} per sample, which puts a 1{,}000-sample dataset beyond
any realistic budget. A two-dimensional simulation runs in
\SI{149}{\minute} but returns a loaded $Q$ too high by a factor of
$11.3$, because it cannot represent vertical confinement.

The construction used instead simulates in three dimensions only the part of
the device that genuinely requires it. A single pass of the directional
coupler is simulated in 3-D FDTD to obtain the power coupling coefficient
$\kappa^2$; the ring response is then assembled analytically from coupled-mode
theory [13,14], with the effective and group indices taken from a 3-D mode
sweep and the propagation losses from a bend-loss sweep. The total cost is
approximately \SI{16}{\hour}.

A five-parameter model is fitted to the coupler simulations,
\begin{equation}
\kappa^2 = \sin^2\theta,
\qquad
\theta = \kappa_0(w)\,e^{-\gamma(w) g}
\left(L_c + B\sqrt{2\pi R/\gamma}\right),
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
in which the gap and the coupling length enter \emph{only} through the single
phase $\theta$. Equation~\eqref{eq:theta} is the origin of the degeneracy
analysed in Section~\ref{sec:o6}, and $\theta$ is the quantity used as a
metric in Section~\ref{sec:metric}. The fitted $B = 1.0211$ is close to its
first-principles value of unity.

The coupler set was built in two stages by augmented Latin hypercube sampling:
48 points, then a further 152, for 200 in total. The extension widened the
$\kappa^2$ range from $0.0021$--$0.3138$ to $0.0019$--$0.4599$ and moved $B$
from $1.0425$ toward unity, with residuals flat across two and a half decades
and no evidence of $\sin^2\theta$ saturation.

The modelling dataset comprises 1{,}150 labelled geometries --- 1{,}000 from a
primary Latin hypercube design plus a 150-point supplementary batch covering a
sparse corner of the space --- split 805/172/173 into training, validation and test sets with a fixed seed. Before modelling, a quality gate removes any row whose exact drop-port half-maximum does not exist, whose peak drop transmission exceeds one, whose responses are not finite or whose $\QL \le 0$; the response bank of the information bound applies the same gate. The test set was held out from model fitting; post-hoc analyses that did use it (for example the neighbourhood size of the information bound) are marked where they occur.

\subsection{Full 3-D validation of the ring model}

\textbf{Resonance and FSR.} $\lres$ is about $0.1\%$ ($0.7$--$2.2$\,nm) below the analytic value; FSR is biased $+2.2$ to $+2.8\%$, identical for all three geometries. The ring's group index is $2.8$--$3.0\%$ below the mode solver's. Round-trip length, bend effects (a 210-point bent-waveguide sweep), FSR extraction and the $n_g$ polynomial were each excluded by measurement. A straight-guide test traces the deficit to the FDTD engine's dispersionless material fit (group index $4.17$--$4.22$ against the solver's $4.329$); a dispersive fit brings it to $+0.3$ to $+1.4\%$. The ring itself was not re-run with that fit, so for the ring this is an inference. The dataset labels use the mode solver, which agrees with the measured device of [11] ($4.344$ against $4.345$ implied).

\textbf{$\QL$.} At low to moderate $\QL$ the analytic model is correct to within 2\% after extrapolation to zero mesh (Table~\ref{tab:ringval}); the 4--6\% error at the production mesh is attributed to mesh error, an inference from two geometries and two meshes. At the highest $\QL$ the simulation lies $17$--$27\%$ below the analytic value and refinement moves it away from the model; the cause is unexplained (a wavelength-blind bend-loss model is one untested candidate).

\textbf{Insertion loss does not validate:} drop-port peak transmission differs by $-12.5\%$, $+1.5\%$ and $-17.9\%$ with no consistent sign.

\textbf{Scope.} Every model was trained and scored on identical labels, so a shared bias cancels from all model-to-model comparisons (O6, the metric result, realisability, sample efficiency). What must be qualified is the absolute accuracy of $\QL$ above about $4300$ ($84.7\%$ of the 1{,}150 labels), of IL anywhere, and the scenario verdicts judged against $\QL$ specifications.


\subsection{Models and protocol}

Four method families are compared, each in the forward and inverse direction,
on the identical split with identical metrics: Random Forest, XGBoost, a plain
feed-forward network, and a tandem network trained through the frozen forward
model. Tree models are evaluated with raw and normalised inputs. The forward network architecture was selected by a 144-configuration grid search over depth, width, learning rate and weight decay; the three best configurations differ by under $0.1\%$ in validation error, so the choice among them is effectively a tie, and the selected network has depth 4 and width 256. The naive and tandem inverse networks use a fixed 64--128--64 topology, deliberately not the search winner, so that the comparison isolates the loss function; they were not tuned. All settings are in Appendix~\ref{sec:appimpl}.

Two metric conventions require statement. Aggregate geometric error is the
root-mean-square relative geometric error
\begin{equation}
\rge = 100\% \times
\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\sum_{p \in \{R,w,g,L_c\}}
\left(\frac{p^{\mathrm{pred}} - p^{\mathrm{target}}}{s_p}\right)^2 },
\label{eq:rge}
\end{equation}
with $s_p = p^{\mathrm{target}}$ for $R$, $w$ and $g$ following [12], and
$s_p = \SI{3}{\micro\metre}$, the range width, for $L_c$ --- necessary because
$L_c = 0$ is a valid design point at which a target-normalised relative error
is undefined. Per-parameter terms and the value over $\{R,w,g\}$ alone (the normalisation of [12] without our $L_c$ term; [12] also has a thickness parameter, which is fixed here) are reported alongside every aggregate.

Closed-loop response error is measured by taking a model's predicted geometry
for each of twenty representative test targets (a hundred for the naive
network, Section~3.2), re-simulating the coupler in
3-D FDTD, reassembling the ring response, and comparing to the response
originally requested. The FDTD step is what makes this independent: feeding
predictions back through the fitted $\kappa^2$ model would merely replay the
model that produced the labels.

The best forward model is the network, at $3.568\%$ mean MAPE across the four
targets against $5.323\%$ for the best XGBoost variant and $11.929\%$ for the
best Random Forest. Table~\ref{tab:fwd} reports all three required metrics for
every target and method.

Objective O2 required $R^2 \ge 0.90$ and MAPE $\le 10\%$ for at least three of
four targets. \textbf{Two of four are met, so O2 is not met.} Two points
about that verdict deserve care.

First, the verdict is reproducible, and it was measured as such. $\lres$ fails
on $R^2$ by an enormous margin and does so consistently; IL fails on MAPE at
$11.331 \pm 1.101\%$ against a $10\%$ bar, with only one seed in five passing.
A single training run is not a sound basis for a hard-threshold verdict, and
we report the distribution rather than a draw from it.

Second, IL's failure is a property of the metric and the target's scale, not of
the model's accuracy. IL is predicted to $0.072$\,dB RMSE with
$R^2 = 0.9966$. But the dataset's IL has a median of $0.524$\,dB, with
$48.3\%$ of rows below $0.5$\,dB and a minimum of $0.032$\,dB, so a constant
absolute error of $0.07$\,dB produces $13.7\%$ relative error at the median
and over $200\%$ at the extreme. Clearing a $10\%$ MAPE bar would require
absolute accuracy better than about $0.052$\,dB on a decibel quantity already
predicted to seven hundredths of a decibel. We report O2 as not met under the
criterion as defined. Whether that verdict carries system-level meaning is a
separate question, and this work can answer it from its own scenario
definitions rather than by appeal to typical practice: Scenario A allows
\SI{1.5}{dB} of insertion loss per ring and Scenario B \SI{1.0}{dB}, so a
\SI{0.072}{dB} RMSE consumes $4.8\%$ and $7.2\%$ of those budgets
respectively. The model is accurate enough for both. A relative-error
threshold was the wrong instrument for a target whose values approach zero,
and we would specify an absolute one --- tied to the link budget, as above
--- if fixing the objectives again.

The resonance wavelength is not learnable by any method here. All four tree
variants return \emph{negative} $R^2$ --- worse than predicting the mean ---
and the network manages $0.0069$. The cause is sensitivity:
$\mathrm{d}\lres/\mathrm{d}R \approx 0.15$\,nm/nm, so placing a resonance to
within \SI{1}{nm} requires the radius to roughly \SI{7}{nm}, about one part in $1.4\times10^3$ of the \SI{10}{\micro\metre} radius range. The consequence propagates: $\lres$ accounts for
almost all of the forward model's residual loss, so the 144-configuration grid
search was in effect ranking configurations on noise.

Objective O6 requires the naive inverse network to be judged against
\emph{both} criteria used by the two sides of the disagreement: a
geometry-space criterion of $R^2 \ge 0.80$, and a response-space criterion of
closed-loop re-simulated error.

\textbf{In geometry space the naive network fails.} Its per-parameter $R^2$
values are $0.9930$ for $R$, $0.2920$ for $w$, $0.6914$ for $g$ and $0.2166$
for $L_c$: one parameter of four clears $0.80$. Figure~\ref{fig:o6} shows
that this is not specific to the network --- every method recovers the radius
and no method recovers the other three.

\textbf{In response space the naive network passes, comfortably.} Re-simulating
its predicted geometries for twenty representative targets gives a
root-mean-square closed-loop response error of $3.669\%$, against a $20\%$
requirement, with all twenty predictions physically realisable. Per target:
$\lres$ $0.203\%$, FSR $0.980\%$, $\QL$ $4.037\%$, IL $3.594\%$.

Twenty targets is a small sample, so we extended the check for the naive network
to a hundred test targets spaced evenly through the test set (twelve of them are
among the original twenty). The result holds, with one correction. The
root-mean-square closed-loop error over the $96$ realisable predictions is
$3.989\%$ (bootstrap 95\% interval $3.36$--$4.62\%$, $B = 10\,000$), and every
target of every realisable design is within $20\%$ of the request, the worst
single error being $17.98\%$. Per target (mean absolute error): $\lres$
$0.212\%$, FSR $0.879\%$, $\QL$ $3.936\%$, IL $4.452\%$. The correction is that
\emph{not all designs are buildable}: four of the hundred predictions fall just
outside the design box (radius $4.87$--$4.99$\,\si{\micro\metre} against a lower
limit of $5$\,\si{\micro\metre}), so the buildable fraction is $96\%$ (Wilson 95\%
interval $90.2$--$98.4\%$). This agrees with the $95.4\%$ of Table~\ref{tab:inv}
and shows that the ``all twenty realisable'' result above was a feature of the small sample.
The $96$ coupler simulations of this run were also not in the $\kappa^2$ fit; the fitted model
sits above them by $3.32\%$ on average (MAPE $4.10\%$, maximum $8.72\%$, all within
$10\%$), consistent with the positive bias of a few per cent noted in Section~2.3.
The other models were not extended and are compared at twenty targets in Section~3.3.

On this device the two criteria give opposite verdicts on the same predictions. That is compatible with, but does not establish, the explanation that the reports of [3] and [7,9] differ in the criterion they apply.

\paragraph{Why: counting constraints.} The structure of the problem explains
the split exactly. Of the four targets, $\lres$ carries essentially no
geometric information, as Section~3.1 established. FSR fixes the round-trip
length $L = 2\pi R + 2L_c$. And $\QL$ and IL both depend on the coupling only
through $\kappa^2 = \sin^2\theta$, so they constrain the single phase $\theta$
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta} and nothing finer. Four targets therefore impose two
effective constraints --- one on $L$, one on $\theta$ --- on four unknowns,
leaving an approximately two-dimensional family of geometries with indistinguishable
responses.

That prediction is quantitatively borne out (Section~\ref{sec:baseline} measures how approximate the degeneracy is). $L$ is dominated by $2\pi R$, so
$R$ is recoverable, and it is ($R^2 = 0.9930$). The remaining three parameters
enter only through $\theta$, and none is individually recoverable
($R^2 = 0.13$--$0.69$ across all methods). Direct confirmation: four
geometries with gaps spanning \SIrange{210}{270}{nm} and coupling lengths
spanning \SIrange{0.209}{2.353}{\micro\metre} share a loaded $Q$ of
$14\,659.8$ to the last digit. The naive network recovers $\theta$ itself to
$1.824\%$, with $96.5\%$ of its predictions within $5\%$ of the correct level
set. It is not failing; it is landing on the right level set and being scored
on its position along it.

\paragraph{A systematic search for degenerate pairs.} The four-geometry example above was found by inspection, so we also searched for such cases systematically. Among all pairs of the $1000$ primary-design rows we kept those whose targets are practically indistinguishable: FSR within $1\%$, $\QL$ within $5\%$, IL within $5\%$ and $\lres$ within $0.5$\,nm. These tolerances are our choice, not derived from a measurement uncertainty. Nineteen pairs qualify. Measured with the $\rge$ convention, the geometric difference between the two members has a median of $19.4\%$, a 90th percentile of $31.4\%$ and a maximum of $45.8\%$; it exceeds $15\%$, the aggregate error of the naive network, in $13$ of the $19$ pairs and exceeds $30\%$ in three. The most different pair has coupling lengths of $2.63$ and $0.18\,\si{\micro\metre}$ and gaps of $293$ and $207$\,nm, yet FSR of $6.786$ and $6.844$\,nm, $\QL$ of $11\,185$ and $11\,550$, and IL of $0.292$ and $0.291$\,dB. A model that sees only the targets must return the same geometry for both members of a pair, so by the triangle inequality at least one of them is missed by roughly half the pair difference (approximately, because each relative error is normalised by its own true value). For the pair above that is about $23\%$, more than the naive network's total error. Pairs are rare because $1000$ points are sparse in a four-dimensional space, so the count shows that degeneracy exists and says nothing about how common it is.

Because this verdict rests on labels produced by the coupled-mode assembly,
and Section~2.4 measured that assembly's group index to sit about $3\%$ below
full 3-D FDTD, it is fair to ask whether a label error of that size could
manufacture or conceal the degeneracy. We tested it directly. The inverse
model is trained on FSR labels rescaled by $\pm 3\%$ and then asked for
geometries matching \emph{unshifted} targets --- the mismatch between the
labels a model learns from and the response a user requests is what a biased
label actually costs. (Shifting both sides instead is invariant: tree splits
rescale with the feature and standardisation absorbs a uniform factor, so no
model in the pipeline can respond to it.) Across $\pm 3\%$ the aggregate
geometric error moves by $0.65$ percentage points and the reconstructed
response error by $0.13$, and the count of parameters reaching
$R^2 \ge 0.80$ never leaves one of four: $R$ stays at $0.958$--$0.961$ while
$w$, $g$ and $L_c$ stay at $0.16$--$0.68$. The degeneracy is structural, as
the constraint-counting argument requires --- a uniform shift in one label
cannot change how many independent constraints four targets impose. This
test uses a plain random-forest inverse as its vehicle, so its absolute
error level is not comparable with Table~\ref{tab:score}; only the
differences across the three label scalings are meaningful. It also probes
the simplest form the label error could take, a uniform scaling, whereas the
measured discrepancy is geometry-dependent with a mild wavelength trend.

Section~\ref{sec:o6} showed that geometric error and response error disagree
about whether a model works. Table~\ref{tab:cl} shows that they also disagree
about which models work, and by how much.

The naive network and XGBoost (normalised) differ by $1.04$ percentage points
in $\rge$ --- $15.506\%$ against $16.546\%$, a difference a reader would
reasonably call negligible. In closed loop they differ by a factor of $3.6$ ($3.669\%$ against $13.329\%$; bootstrap 95\% interval for the ratio $2.3$--$6.3$ over the twenty targets). Had only $\rge$ been measured, these two
models would have been called equivalent, and the conclusion would have been
wrong by nearly a factor of four on the quantity that determines whether a design works. Closed-loop error is computed from twenty test targets spaced evenly through the test set; bootstrap 95\% intervals on the RMS are $2.4$--$5.1\%$ (naive DNN), $3.4$--$5.7\%$ (RF raw), $9.1$--$17.1\%$ (XGBoost norm) and $2.3$--$8.7\%$ (tandem, 8 targets).

The degeneracy suggests why, and $\theta$ makes it measurable. An error that
moves along a $\theta$ level set yields a different valid design; one that
moves across it yields a wrong response. Figure~\ref{fig:metric} tests this
on the four models whose predictions were re-simulated. Closed-loop error is
proportional to $\theta$ error, with the four ratios spanning
$1.87$--$2.24$, a factor of $1.20$. The same ratios formed with $\rge$ are
$0.20$--$0.81$, a factor of $4.00$. Part of this is expected: because $\kappa^2 = \sin^2\theta$, a small relative error in $\theta$ produces roughly twice that relative error in $\kappa^2$, and hence in $\QL$ and IL. The measured ratios (about $2.1$) are consistent with that. The result rests on four models and is a consistency check on the constraint-counting argument, not an independent statistical test.

Two features of that comparison matter for its strength. The first is that
model family is controlled: Random Forest (raw) is an ensemble method with
$\theta$ error $2.095\%$ and lands at $4.589\%$, beside the network's
$3.669\%$ and far from XGBoost's $13.329\%$. Within the tree family alone,
$\theta$ error varies by a factor $3.4$ and closed-loop error by $2.9$, while
$\rge$ differs by $3\%$ --- so there is no architecture explanation available.
The second is that ordering alone would not have discriminated: $\rge$ happens to order five of the six model pairs correctly (it ranks the tandem network worst, whereas in closed loop XGBoost is worst). What it cannot do is say how
much worse one is than another, which is what a design metric is for.

\subsection{A direct optimiser as a baseline, and the source of the error floor}
\label{sec:baseline}

Whether a learned inverse model is needed at all can be tested directly. We solved each of the twenty closed-loop targets by differential evolution on the analytic ring model, minimising the RMS relative error of the four responses over the design box (population of $120$, no training data, seconds per target). In the model, all twenty targets were matched to better than $0.02\%$ (median $8\times 10^{-5}\%$); the two whose targets lie at the edge of the reachable range were matched to $0.018\%$ and $0.007\%$. Re-simulated through the same 3-D FDTD coupler and analytic ring as in Table~\ref{tab:cl}, these designs give an RMS closed-loop error of $2.89\%$ (bootstrap 95\% interval $2.32$--$3.42\%$, $B = 10\,000$), against $3.67\%$ ($2.35$--$5.11\%$) for the naive network on the same targets. The unpaired intervals overlap. Paired over the same twenty targets (bootstrap of the difference), the optimiser's RMS error is lower than the naive network's by $0.78$ points ($95\%$ interval $-2.35$ to $+0.64$; lower on $10$ of $20$ targets), so the two are not separable, and lower than the bounded and the bounded, weighted tandem networks' by $1.47$ points ($-2.40$ to $-0.52$; $14$ of $20$ targets) and $1.02$ points ($-1.95$ to $-0.09$; $14$ of $20$ targets). The optimiser is also lower than Random Forest (raw) by $1.70$ points ($-3.17$ to $-0.34$) and than XGBoost (normalised) by $10.4$ points ($-14.3$ to $-6.2$; XGBoost's closed-loop error is $13.3\%$). With five comparisons and a single initialisation of each tandem network, the intervals that exclude zero, other than XGBoost's, are suggestive, not conclusive. Twenty targets are also few: at $80\%$ power they detect a difference of about $2.2$ points between the optimiser and the naive network and about $1.4$ points between the optimiser and the tandem variants, so `not separable' from the naive network means `not powered'; the observed $0.78$ points would need about $160$ targets. The interval does bound the other direction, though: the naive network is better than the optimiser by at most $0.64$ points (upper end of the interval). The networks therefore show no accuracy advantage over the optimiser larger than the sizes quoted here. The $\lres$ and FSR errors are about $0.005\%$ (rms); essentially all of the error is in $\QL$ ($3.97\%$ rms) and IL ($4.20\%$ rms). The networks' remaining advantage is evaluation cost, which matters little here because the analytic ring model is itself cheap.

That error is the discrepancy between the fitted coupler model and 3-D FDTD. At the twenty optimiser designs the ratio of the FDTD to the model $\kappa^2$ has median $0.994$ and a standard deviation of its logarithm of $0.045$ (range $0.93$--$1.08$), and no geometric parameter correlates with it significantly (all $|r| \le 0.31$ with $n = 20$). A scatter of that size in $\kappa^2$ is of the size of the $\QL$ and IL errors above. This is consistent with the validation of the coupler model in Table~\ref{tab:valid} (cross-validated MAPE $3.36\%$; out-of-sample MAPE $1.23$--$5.16\%$), and the twenty designs are further out-of-sample tests of it. It is scatter, not a removable bias: neither a constant factor nor a linear fit in the four parameters is supported by these twenty points. We have not separated how much of it is model error and how much is FDTD numerical noise (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). We therefore report the closed-loop error of about $3\%$ as the fidelity limit of the coupler model, not as a property of the inverse method.

The degeneracy can be quantified the same way. At the twenty designs we took the Jacobian of the logarithms of the four targets with respect to the four parameters, each scaled to its range. The median singular values are $5.5$, $1.1$, $0.13$ and $0.027$. The map is therefore locally full rank in the strict sense, but two directions are to the responses $40$ to $200$ times less visible than the strongest: moving along them across a parameter's entire range changes the targets by about $13\%$ and $2.7\%$. The family of equivalent designs is thus approximately two-dimensional at a response tolerance above $13\%$ and approximately one-dimensional, mostly a combination of $L_c$ and $g$ at constant coupling, at about $3\%$. The analysis is local and linear, and one of the twenty designs behaves differently (its flattest direction is dominated by $w$), so we quote medians.

Does the choice among equivalent designs matter for fabrication tolerance? For each target we found up to $60$ distinct designs meeting the four targets to within $0.1\%$ (and a second set to within $1\%$, below the coupler-model scatter) and applied the same random width and gap errors to every one of them ($200$ draws; $\sigma_w = 0.7$\,nm and $\sigma_g = 1$\,nm, assumed values that give a resonance spread of about $0.5$\,nm). The best design's resonance-shift spread was smaller than the median design's by $18.5\%$ (median over targets; $22.7\%$ for the $1\%$ set; worst-to-best ratio $1.5$--$1.6$; $1$ of $19$ targets above $30\%$), while the spreads of $\QL$ and IL differed by only $0.2\%$ and $0.1\%$. The mechanism is the width. Members of a family differ in width, and the resonance shift per nanometre of width error falls as the waveguide widens, in the direction of wafer-scale measurements in which wider waveguides show much lower variation in effective and group index [19] (e.g.\ \SI{480}{nm} against \SI{1200}{nm} waveguides, wider than our box): a $0.7$\,nm error shifts the resonance by $0.72$, $0.62$, $0.54$ and $0.41$\,nm at $w = 400$, $425$, $450$ and $499$\,nm (a fifth point, at $475$\,nm, jumped by one free spectral range between resonance orders and is not used). Within the design box the best a designer can do is therefore about a quarter below the mid-range value, which is what the sampled families already reach. Gap errors do not move the resonance in this model. At an unrealistically large $\sigma_w = 5$\,nm the shift exceeds half the free spectral range, the spread saturates, and the difference between designs disappears ($4.3\%$); we regard that setting as invalid for the resonance. Thickness changes this. A mode-solver sweep of the straight guide at $215$, $220$ and $225$\,nm gives a resonance shift of $1.33$--$1.36$\,nm per nanometre of thickness, almost independent of width, against $0.94$ falling to $0.63$\,nm per nanometre of width over the same range. To first order, with $\sigma_w = 0.7$\,nm the best-versus-median saving is $16\%$ for width variation alone, but $1.8\%$ with a thickness spread of $1$\,nm and $0.1\%$ with $2$\,nm (no target reaches $30\%$ in any case; thickness spread is a swept parameter here, not a measured value). The benefit of choosing the widest equivalent design therefore exists only where width variation dominates, and disappears once thickness variation of $1$\,nm or more is present. The gap-dependent coupler phase and the effect of thickness on the coupling are not modelled, so this estimates one mechanism and is not a fabrication yield.

\subsection{Physical realisability}
\label{sec:build}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{fig/fig5_buildable.pdf}
\caption{Fraction of test-set predictions lying inside the design box. The
distinction is architectural, and invisible to both metrics of
Section~\ref{sec:metric}.}
\label{fig:build}
\end{figure}

Neither $\rge$ nor $\theta$ error detects a prediction that cannot be
fabricated, and the differences between architectures here are large.

The tandem network produced unbuildable geometries for twelve of the twenty
closed-loop targets: eleven with negative coupling length, down to
\SI{-0.881}{\micro\metre}, and one with a width of \SI{397.8}{nm}. Over the full test set its buildable fraction is $33.5\%$ at the reported initialisation, and $11.6$--$83.8\%$ over five initialisations (Section~\ref{sec:methodcmp}). The mechanism is visible in
its loss: the tandem network is penalised only through the frozen forward
surrogate, which will evaluate $L_c = \SI{-0.88}{\micro\metre}$ and return a
plausible response. Nothing in the objective encodes that the design space has
edges. The naive network, which regresses against real geometries and is
therefore pulled toward a region where all targets are valid, leaves the box on
$4.6\%$ of the test set.

Both tree families produced buildable geometries in every case. For Random
Forest this is guaranteed rather than fortunate: its prediction is a mean of
leaf values and hence a convex combination of training targets, so it lies in
their convex hull, and the design box is convex. Gradient boosting is additive
rather than convex and carries no such guarantee, but violated the box
nowhere here.

The practical consequence is that $\theta$ error and realisable fraction are
complementary and both are needed; neither subsumes the other. The tandem
network illustrates why. On its buildable predictions its closed-loop error is
$5.936\%$, better than XGBoost's, and its $\theta$ error is a respectable
$2.654\%$. Judged on those numbers alone it looks like a competitive model.
Sixty per cent of its designs cannot be made.

Because a curve read at a single random seed cannot distinguish an ordering
from initialisation noise, the whole sweep was repeated over five seeds
(42--46) and is reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation (divisor $n$; with divisor $n-1$ every spread in this section would be $11.8\%$ larger). Two methods are
called separated only if a Welch test and an exact permutation test over the five
seeds both give $p<0.05$ after Holm correction within each training size
(\texttt{p8\_seedstats.py}). With five seeds per group the permutation test cannot
give $p$ below $0.0079$, so a pair can be separated only if all five seeds of one
method beat all five of the other. The earlier pooled-spread rule (gap larger than
$\sqrt{s_a^2+s_b^2}$) gives the same verdict on every pair reported below. The hyperparameters of every model in the sweep are those found for the full training pool and are reused at every size; the tree models are the normalised-input variants with the seed varied, so their full-size means ($5.115\%$ for XGBoost, $11.802\%$ for the forward random forest) are seed averages and differ slightly from the single-run values of Table~\ref{tab:fwd} ($5.323\%$ and $11.929\%$).

In the forward direction at the full 805-sample pool the network is most
accurate, at $3.776 \pm 0.282\%$ against XGBoost's $5.115 \pm 0.115\%$: a gap
of $1.34$ against a pooled spread of $0.30$, so the ordering holds across seeds (Welch $p=0.0002$, permutation $p=0.0079$, Holm-adjusted $p=0.024$). Below
300 samples it is \emph{not}. XGBoost averages $12.815\%$ and the network
$14.303\%$, a gap of $1.49$ against a pooled spread of $3.09$, driven by the
network's seed-to-seed variability at small $n$ ($\pm 3.07$ at $n = 100$).
The two are comparable in that regime and we do not claim a winner (at $n=100$ and $200$ the network--XGBoost difference has Welch $p=0.19$ and $0.75$, not separated; at $n=400$ the network is ahead and separated, at $n=600$ not separated again). A
single-seed reading of the same sweep shows XGBoost ahead by $4.4$ points and
would support the opposite, stronger statement; it does not survive repetition.

In the inverse direction the naive network is lowest at the smallest and the largest training sizes ($16.475\%$ against the random forest's $16.875\%$ below 300 samples; $15.500 \pm 0.069\%$ against $15.853 \pm 0.016\%$ at full size), separated over seeds by the test above (Holm-corrected $p=0.048$ in each block, the smallest attainable with five seeds). At $n=400$ and $600$ the random forest is lower than the naive network by $0.19$ and $0.26$ points, also separated over seeds. Two cautions apply. The random forest's own curve is not monotone ($15.473\%$ at $n = 600$, $15.853\%$ at $n = 805$, a change of $0.38$ percentage points), which is as large as the margins above, so the ordering is established across initialisations but not across draws of the data. And test-set sampling uncertainty is larger than seed spread: 95\% bootstrap intervals over the 173 test rows are about $\pm 1.0$ to $1.2$ percentage points for every model (naive DNN $15.51\%$, interval $14.47$--$16.52\%$; RF (raw) $16.04\%$, $14.96$--$17.12\%$). Paired over the same resampled rows, the naive network's advantage at full size is $-0.53$ points against RF (raw) (interval $-1.23$ to $+0.14$), $-0.33$ against RF (norm) ($-1.12$ to $+0.45$), $-0.95$ against XGBoost (raw) ($-1.94$ to $+0.06$) and $-1.04$ against XGBoost (norm) ($-2.04$ to $-0.03$). Only the last interval excludes zero, and only just, so at full size the naive network is not shown to be more accurate than the random forest on this test set; it is clearly more accurate than the tandem network ($-13.96$ points, interval $-15.72$ to $-12.04$). The O3 shortfall ($15.5\%$ against $15\%$) lies inside the naive network's own interval (bootstrap probability that its $\rge$ is at most $15\%$: $0.17$), so we report it as ``not met at the point estimate'', not as missed by a fixed number of standard deviations.

The tandem network's inverse curve is not a learning curve at all:
$34.6 \pm 8.6$, $39.5 \pm 7.0$, $87.1 \pm 19.3$, $117.5 \pm 38.0$ and
$29.1 \pm 6.7$ per cent across the five sizes --- non-monotonic in training
size, with a standard deviation of up to about a third of the mean. This is
consistent with the box-escape mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:build}; with
nothing anchoring predictions inside the design space, where the network
wanders is largely a function of initialisation and data. The tandem
architecture is the standard remedy for one-to-many ambiguity in this
literature, and, as implemented here --- unbounded outputs, and a loss in which $\lres$ contributes $99.3\%$ of the response-space gradient --- it is worse than the naive network and its error is erratic across seeds. Whether this reflects the architecture or this implementation of it was tested directly. Four tandem variants were trained over the same five seeds (42--46) through the same frozen forward model (Table~\ref{tab:tandemfix}): the baseline above; a variant with a sigmoid output, so that every prediction lies inside the design box; a variant in which each target's squared error is divided by the forward model's own validation error on that target, which has no free parameter and lowers the weight of $\lres$ to $0.003$ of the mean; and both together.

Bounding the output removes both faults of the baseline: every design is buildable, and the seed-to-seed spread of $\rge$ falls from $5.79$ to $0.29$ percentage points. It does not close the gap in geometry space: the bounded tandem network stays $3.96$ points above the naive network, against a pooled seed spread of $0.30$. Weighting alone leaves the geometry unchanged ($31.35 \pm 7.74\%$, $30.6\%$ buildable) but improves the reconstructed response, from $4.34\%$ to $3.31\%$, mostly through $\QL$. With both changes, the coupling-phase error ($1.92 \pm 0.11\%$) and the reconstructed-response error ($3.61\%$) are comparable to the naive network's ($1.82\%$ and $3.81\%$, single run), while $\rge$ remains about four points higher. A bounded, weighted tandem network is therefore not worse than the naive network in response space or in $\theta$; it is worse only in geometry space, which is the distinction this paper is about. These runs are not the sweep of Figure~\ref{fig:sample}: that sweep retrains the forward network for every seed and initialises the tandem network at seeds 43--47, so its full-size tandem spread ($29.1 \pm 6.7\%$) is a different experiment, with one run in common ($29.461\%$). We also re-simulated the bounded network and the bounded, weighted network in closed loop (seed 43, the same twenty test targets as Table~\ref{tab:cl}), through the same 3-D FDTD coupler and analytic ring. All twenty designs are buildable for both, and the RMS closed-loop response error is $4.36\%$ (bootstrap 95\% interval $3.46$--$5.24\%$) for the bounded network and $3.91\%$ ($3.26$--$4.52\%$) for the bounded, weighted one, against $3.67\%$ ($2.4$--$5.1\%$) for the naive network on the same targets. The intervals overlap, so with twenty targets and one initialisation we cannot separate the three; what the result shows is that a bounded, weighted tandem network, which is about four points worse in geometry space, is not measurably worse in closed loop.

A final observation on metric dependence. Ranking parameter difficulty by mean
$R^2$ gives $L_c > w > g > R$, hardest first; ranking by mean $\rge$
contribution gives $L_c > g > w > R$. The two orderings disagree on $w$
against $g$. Under either, the coupling gap is \emph{not} the hardest
parameter --- the coupling length is, with mean $R^2 = -0.2645$ --- so the coupling gap does not dominate the error.

\subsection{An information bound}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/fig4_information_floor.pdf}
\caption{Left: the information bound against response-bank size, with the best

objective threshold. Right: per-parameter error of the best model against the
bound estimator's per-parameter error; these are not individual bounds, since the estimator minimises the aggregate. Both panels use the same bank and $k = 5$ ($14.323\%$).}
\label{fig:floor}
\end{figure}

Objective O3 required, for at least one method, $\rge \le 15\%$ \emph{and} closed-loop error $\le 20\%$. The closed-loop half is met ($3.669\%$); the best aggregate $\rge$ is $15.506\%$. \textbf{O3 is therefore not met}, by $0.506$ percentage points.

Whether that shortfall reflects the models or the problem depends on how much geometric information four scalar targets contain. We draw a bank of $10^6$ distinct geometries over the design box, label it with the same analytic responder and quality gate, average for each test target the $k$ bank geometries with the closest responses, and compute the $\rge$ of that average. This is the error of one specific predictor, so it is an \emph{upper bound} on the Bayes-optimal error: a value below a threshold proves the true bound is below it; a value above proves nothing. The estimate falls from $16.063\%$ at $N = 12\,500$ to $14.323\%$ at $N = 10^6$ (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}), below $15\%$.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{At $N = 10^6$ the estimate is $14.323\%$, below the $15\%$ threshold; we therefore have no evidence that the threshold is unreachable. We do not claim it is reachable.} The neighbourhood size $k$ was chosen on the same 173 test targets and the $k$-curve is not smooth ($14.32\%$ at $k = 5$, $14.73\%$ at $k = 10$). The 95\% bootstrap interval over test targets, $[13.18, 15.42]\%$ ($[13.05, 15.32]\%$ with $k$ re-chosen per resample), contains the threshold; the estimate is below $15\%$ in $89\%$ of resamples. Three independent banks of the same size give $14.41$, $14.77$ and $14.56\%$ at their best $k$ (all prefer $k = 25$) and $14.94$, $14.81$ and $15.08\%$ at $k = 5$, so the original $14.32\%$ is the lowest of four and a fixed $k = 5$ crosses $15\%$ in one fresh bank. The margin is about $0.4$ points, against a bank-to-bank s.d.\ of $0.15$--$0.2$.
\end{quote}

The estimate is not converged: the optimal $k$ drifts from $25$ to $5$ across the sweep and the descent accelerates ($0.096$ to $0.718$ points per e-fold of bank size), so the estimator's finite-sample bias is still unwinding, no rate can be read from the sweep, and a three-parameter asymptotic fit is not identifiable. The per-parameter values in Figure~\ref{fig:floor} are not individual bounds, because the estimator minimises the aggregate. Finally, enlarging the training set with rows from the \emph{fitted} analytic model lowers measured $\rge$ below $15\%$, but those rows add sampling density, not physics, so we do not report O3 as met by that route.

Objective O7 asked which methods meet a CPO scenario's specification within its stated tolerance. Each candidate's coupler was re-simulated in 3-D FDTD; the ring response was then assembled analytically, so the verdicts inherit the biases of Section~\ref{sec:ringval}. Table~\ref{tab:cpo} answers in three parts.

\textbf{(i) Given point targets, the trained models fail.} Eleven of the twelve failures involve resonance placement. The single exception is Random Forest (raw) in Scenario A, which placed the resonance at \SI{1548.058}{nm}, inside the $\pm\SI{2.0}{nm}$ window, and then missed FSR by \SI{0.13}{nm}. The naive network's closed-loop $\lres$ error is \SI{3.15}{nm} mean absolute error, \SI{4.33}{nm} RMS and \SI{10.9}{nm} at worst; always predicting the dataset's mean resonance wavelength gives \SI{2.66}{nm} RMS on the same twenty targets, and a randomly chosen geometry about \SI{4.4}{nm}.

\textbf{(ii) The point targets themselves are not attainable.} The four scenario numbers (resonance, FSR, $\QL$, IL) were passed to the models as one tuple. A differential-evolution search on the analytic ring model, minimising the RMS relative error to that tuple, reaches a best of $21$--$26\%$ for the four tuples tested (six restarts agree), and it gives up on the resonance, because \SI{5}{nm} is only $0.3\%$ relative error while the other three quantities are off by tens of percent. A resonance miss of several nm is therefore what any point-target method returns for an unattainable tuple, and the data do not separate that from the networks' own error. We do not claim the networks are better or worse on the resonance; we claim the test of (i) cannot decide it.

\textbf{(iii) As a feasibility problem, both scenarios are met.} The scenarios are bands and bounds, so the direct question is whether any geometry satisfies all of them. A penalty counting only violations of the resonance window, minimum FSR, $\QL$ band and IL limit reaches zero in under ten seconds for both scenarios, with and without a margin, and all six restarts agree. The four designs were re-simulated with a fresh 3-D FDTD coupler run (energy sum $0.9999$--$1.0000$) and pass (Table~\ref{tab:cpo}, DE rows). The Scenario A designs have $\lres$ within \SI{0.34}{nm} of \SI{1550}{nm}, inside the $\pm\SI{2.0}{nm}$ window; the Scenario B designs within \SI{0.17}{nm}, inside $\pm\SI{0.4}{nm}$.

What this does \emph{not} establish is that a fabricated device would meet the window. The ring response is analytic and its resonance carries a model uncertainty of about $\pm\SI{1.55}{nm}$ against full-ring FDTD ($0.1\%$ of \SI{1550}{nm}; the ten full-ring resonances lie \SIrange{0.7}{2.2}{nm} below the analytic value, and the largest offset exceeds the Scenario A half-window of \SI{2.0}{nm}): $0.78$ of the Scenario A window and $3.9$ times the Scenario B window. The B passes are passes \emph{in the model}; they are not certified. The loaded $Q$ of the B designs ($11\,873$ and $19\,047$) also exceeds the range over which $\QL$ was validated. Independently, hitting Scenario A with fabricated geometry would require the radius controlled to about \SI{13}{nm} and Scenario B to about \SI{3}{nm}, using $\mathrm{d}\lres/\mathrm{d}R \approx 0.15$\,nm/nm, which is below realistic lithographic tolerance. Absolute resonance position is therefore set after fabrication by thermal or carrier trimming, whichever inverse method produced the geometry; inverse design supplies the FSR, loaded $Q$ and insertion loss and a starting resonance close to the channel.

\subsection{What the trimming costs}
\label{sec:trim}

That requirement is only useful if the trimming it demands is practical, so
we quantify the demand. The thermo-optic sensitivity is solved for rather than
assumed: each material index is perturbed in turn and the mode re-solved,
giving $\Gamma_i = \partial n_{\mathrm{eff}} / \partial n_i$ directly.
Perturbing both materials together reproduces the sum of the separate
perturbations to within $0.17\%$ across all six widths, so the response is
linear and the two sensitivities combine. With the literature
$\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}T$ of silicon and oxide --- the same class of input as
the Palik index data used throughout --- and $n_g$ from the mode sweep,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\lres}{\mathrm{d}T}
= \frac{\lambda}{n_g}\left[\Gamma_{\mathrm{Si}}
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{Si}}}{\mathrm{d}T}
+ \Gamma_{\mathrm{SiO_2}}
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{SiO_2}}}{\mathrm{d}T}\right],
\end{equation}

which evaluates to \SIrange{0.0700}{0.0734}{nm/K} over
\SIrange{400}{500}{nm} of width. Published silicon microring tuning is
\SIrange{0.07}{0.08}{nm/K}; nothing here was fitted to that, so the agreement
is an independent check.

Two physical constraints govern the budget. A thermo-optic heater shifts a
resonance to the red only, and the resonance comb repeats every FSR, so a
ring is tuned onto whichever resonance is nearest above it. A ring designed
with a deliberate blue bias $\delta$ therefore needs a shift of
$(\delta - e_i) \bmod \mathrm{FSR}_i$, where $e_i$ is its closed-loop $\lres$
error. Choosing $\delta$ to minimise the worst case is a covering problem on
a circle of circumference FSR, solved here by direct search. The requirement is bounded by one FSR --- up to about \SI{227}{K} for the largest FSR among these geometries, about \SI{135}{K} at the mean --- however
poorly a model places $\lres$.

\section{Discussion}

The central result is that, on this device, a question treated as empirical in the inverse-design literature depends on a definition. Asking whether a naive
inverse network ``converges acceptably'' has no answer until one says in which
space the error is measured, and the two available answers differ here by a
factor of four on the same predictions. For a degenerate design space the
geometry-space question is not merely harder, it is the wrong question: a
model that lands on the correct level set has produced a valid design, and
penalising its position along that set measures nothing a user cares about.

That observation generalises beyond microrings. Wherever the forward map
compresses several geometric degrees of freedom into fewer physical ones ---
which is to say, wherever inverse design is interesting --- the same split
should be expected, and the recommendation follows directly: report
closed-loop response error, and report it alongside a physically meaningful
invariant of the degeneracy rather than a raw geometric distance. Our $\theta$
is such an invariant for this device; the general construction is to identify
the quantity through which the degenerate parameters act and measure error in
it. Ranking designs of similar performance by their robustness to fabrication perturbations has been proposed in a general computational-design setting [20]; Section~\ref{sec:baseline} estimates it, within the model, for one device.

That a direct optimiser matches the networks needs one qualification. It searches the fitted coupler model, which is itself a surrogate built from the $200$ three-dimensional FDTD simulations, and the networks are trained on the same labelled geometries. Learning an inverse map is therefore worth its cost when no cheap forward surrogate exists, or when many targets must be served at negligible marginal cost, and not as a way to gain accuracy over optimisation on a cheap model. This agrees with the caution in [2] that problems with few free parameters (three or four) may be better solved by conventional optimisation than by deep learning.

The realisability result adds a second axis that neither error metric sees. It
also puts a specific cost on the tandem architecture. The tandem network was
introduced [3] precisely to handle degeneracy, and in response space it does:
its closed-loop error on buildable designs, $5.936\%$, is better than
XGBoost's. But measuring the loss only through a surrogate removes the sole
pressure keeping predictions inside the manufacturable domain, and two thirds of its designs leave it in this implementation. A tandem network for a bounded design space needs an
explicit constraint, and [3] does not report the realisable
fraction. A sigmoid-bounded variant confirms it: $100\%$ of its designs are buildable over five seeds, and its $\rge$ spread falls from $5.79$ to $0.29$ points (Section~\ref{sec:methodcmp}).

The information bound changes how a missed threshold should be read. $\rge$
targets in this literature are quoted without reference to how much geometric
information the chosen targets contain --- [12] reports $5.14\%$ (analytical) and $3.46\%$ (FDTD) for one target design under its four-parameter $\{R,w,h,g\}$ convention, against $8.878\%$ here over $\{R,w,g\}$ on $173$ test targets, in a design space whose radius differs by one to two orders of magnitude and which includes a thickness parameter. Such numbers are not commensurable, and a bound
computed per design space is what makes them interpretable. Here the bound
establishes that the shortfall is addressable; in another design space the
same computation might show a target to be unreachable, which is equally
useful to know before pursuing it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{The ring validation is limited.} It is not mesh-converged (a third mesh exceeds the \SI{15.7}{GB} of memory, so the extrapolation rests on two meshes), covers small radius only (ten runs at $R = 5.10$--$\SI{5.20}{\micro\metre}$; a \SI{10}{\micro\metre} ring needs about \SI{20}{GB}), establishes $\QL$ only below about $4300$ to within 2\% ($84.7\%$ of labels lie above), and does not validate IL. The ring's group index is about $3\%$ below the mode solver's; the cause was found and removed on a straight guide, not on the ring, and the coupler $\kappa^2$ values used the same material fit (Section~2.4, Appendix~\ref{sec:appring}). Bend loss is not resolvable by the eigenmode route (the imaginary part of $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is at the solver's numerical floor), so the wavelength-independent bend-loss model stands uncorrected.
\item \textbf{External validation covers FSR only.} The coupling model, bend-loss model, $\QL$ and IL are not validated against measurement. Label fidelity was tested only against a uniform $\pm3\%$ shift (Section~3.2); a geometry-dependent label error would require regenerating the dataset from full 3-D simulation.
\item \textbf{No fabrication.} Every result is simulation-based. The trimming requirement follows from the model's resonance uncertainty and the fabrication sensitivities; the Scenario B passes are model-level passes. The ring response in the closed-loop and scenario evaluations is analytic; only the coupler is re-simulated.
\item \textbf{Small samples.} Closed-loop verification uses twenty targets for four of six models (only the naive network was extended to a hundred); at $80\%$ power the smallest detectable difference is about $1.4$--$2.2$ percentage points (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}), so smaller differences are not resolved. The optimiser baseline, the $\kappa^2$ comparison and the Jacobian analysis share the same twenty targets. Seed tests use five seeds per group: after Holm correction the smallest attainable $p$ is $0.048$, so a pair is separated only if every seed of one method beats every seed of the other, and a larger repetition could separate pairs we call comparable. Seed tests do not cover data-draw or test-set variability (173 targets for geometric error, 20 for closed-loop error); bootstrap intervals are given where computed. Whether the $4.5\%$ scatter in $\kappa^2$ is model error or FDTD noise has not been separated. Hyperparameters were tuned once, at the full training pool, and reused at every size of the sample-efficiency sweep, which may disadvantage all models at small sizes; the inverse networks were not tuned.
\item \textbf{The information bound is not converged.} It is a valid upper bound at every bank size, which suffices for the one-directional conclusion, but its asymptote is not determined, and it depends on the bank draw (s.d.\ $0.15$--$0.2$ points) and on a neighbourhood size chosen on the same test targets.
\item \textbf{$\lres$ is unlearnable within this parameterisation.} An alternative target (resonance offset from a grid wavelength, azimuthal mode order) might be learnable; one candidate showed $|\mathrm{corr}(\lres, g)| = 0.095$ within matched sets, so $\lres$ is close to redundant with FSR for geometry recovery. A systematic study was not performed.
\item \textbf{One device family and coupler symmetry.} Both couplers share a gap and coupling length; asymmetric couplers would change the degeneracy structure. The equivalent-design study uses the analytic model with thickness to first order for the resonance only, no gap-dependent coupler phase, and assumed width, gap and thickness spreads ($0.7$\,nm and $1$\,nm).
\item \textbf{The headline tandem baseline has unbounded outputs and an unweighted loss dominated by $\lres$.} Bounded and weighted variants were evaluated for geometry, $\theta$ error and buildable fraction (Section~\ref{sec:methodcmp}) and, for two of them, in closed loop at a single initialisation (seed 43, twenty targets); the closed-loop figures in Table~\ref{tab:cl} are for the baseline at a single initialisation.
\item \textbf{No independent expert review} of the physical methods was obtained prior to this draft.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Conclusion}

For a four-parameter add-drop silicon microring resonator, a naive inverse
neural network fails the geometry-space convergence criterion and passes the
response-space one. On this device the two error criteria therefore give opposite verdicts on the same predictions; whether that also accounts for the differing reports in the literature [3,7,9] was not tested. The reason is an approximately two-dimensional degeneracy
that follows from counting constraints: four scalar targets impose two
effective conditions, one on round-trip length and one on a single coupling
phase.

A direct optimiser (differential evolution on the analytic model, no training) reaches a closed-loop error no worse than the networks' ($2.89\%$ against $3.67\%$ on the same twenty targets), so on this problem the inverse step is not the limit; the limit is the coupler model's fidelity ($4.5\%$ scatter in $\kappa^2$ against FDTD). Three consequences for practice follow. Geometric error does not predict
response error --- two models within $1.04$ percentage points of each other in
$\rge$ differ by a factor of $3.6$ in closed loop, while error in the coupling
phase predicts closed-loop error to within a factor $1.20$ across three
architecture families. Physical realisability is a separate and
architecture-dependent property that neither metric detects, ranging from a
provable $100\%$ for Random Forest to $33.5\%$ for a tandem network at the reported initialisation (and $100\%$ once its outputs are bounded). And an
information bound computed per design space is what makes an accuracy target
interpretable; here the point estimate gives no evidence that the missed $15\%$ threshold is fundamental.

For co-packaged optics specifically, two conclusions follow. First, the scenarios are attainable: posed as constraints rather than as a point target, a direct optimiser finds geometries that pass a 3-D FDTD coupler re-simulation in both, also after the $\QL$ and IL bias measured in full-ring simulation (for Scenario B an extrapolated bias), the Scenario A design also passes a complete 3-D ring simulation, and the point-target misses of trained models should not be read as a physical limit. Second, the ring model's resonance uncertainty ($\pm1.55$\,nm) exceeds the eight-channel window by a factor of $3.9$, and fabricated geometry varies more than that, so channel assignment is achieved by post-fabrication trimming whichever method produced the geometry.

\section{Full 3-D validation of the ring model: procedure and details}
\label{sec:appring}

The checks above validate the \emph{coupler} and the \emph{mode solver}. The
coupled-mode assembly that turns $\kappa^2$, $n_g$ and loss into $\QL$ and IL
is not tested by either, and closed-loop re-verification does not test it
because it re-simulates the coupler and then recomputes the ring with the same
analytic model that produced the labels. Since $\QL$ and IL carry the
degeneracy argument of Section~\ref{sec:o6}, we simulated the \emph{complete
ring} in 3-D FDTD.

Nine runs were performed: three geometries at $R = \SI{5.20}{\micro\metre}$,
$w = \SI{450}{nm}$, spanning the coupling range, each at the production
\SI{32}{nm} mesh, at a coarse \SI{40}{nm} mesh, and at \SI{40}{nm} with
three times denser spectral sampling as a control on the extraction. The
simulation window was sized from the predicted $\QL$ so that the linewidth
exceeds the transform limit of the DFT window by at least a factor three, and
auto-shutoff was disabled so that the window is exactly the one assumed. All
nine met the resolution criteria (6.1--52.9 points across the FWHM,
3.14--4.12 times the transform limit).

Table~\ref{tab:ringval} (main text) gives the three geometries.

Three findings follow, and they are not all favourable.

\textbf{Resonance wavelength and free spectral range.} $\lres$ is about $0.1\%$ ($0.7$--$2.2$\,nm) below the analytic value across all nine runs. FSR is biased $+2.2$ to $+2.8\%$, the same sign and magnitude every
time. Since FSR $= \lambda^2/(n_g L)$ and $L = 2\pi R + 2L_c$ is exact
geometry --- the ring is built as two straights of length $L_c$ joined by two
\SI{180}{\degree} bends --- the discrepancy must lie in $n_g$: the product
$n_g L$ the model uses differs from the ring's by about $2.5\%$.

The obvious explanation is that the model takes $n_g$ from a
\emph{straight-waveguide} mode sweep while a \SI{5.2}{\micro\metre} bend is
not a straight waveguide. \emph{That explanation does not survive
measurement.} A 210-point bent-waveguide FDE sweep (six radii from
\SIrange{5}{15}{\micro\metre}, six widths, five wavelengths, \SI{10}{nm}
mesh, PML on all boundaries) gives $n_g$ of the bent guide within $0.08\%$ of
the straight guide at every point in the grid, and $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ within
$0.05\%$. The bend contribution is roughly thirty times too small to account
for the observed bias.

Stated as a percentage of FSR this comparison is treacherous, because FSR
itself varies by about $4\%$ across \SIrange{1500}{1600}{nm}; a model FSR
evaluated at $\lres$ and a measured spacing whose resonance pair is centred
\SI{8}{nm} away are not the same quantity. We therefore compare group
indices at matched wavelengths. Each adjacent resonance pair gives
$n_g = \lambda_1\lambda_2 / [L(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)]$, which we compare with
the FDE value interpolated at that pair's midpoint. Over ten pairs spanning the three geometries, \textbf{the ring's group index is $2.8$--$3.0\%$ below the mode solver's}: $-2.83\%$ (s.d.\ $0.24\%$) when the solver's $n_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)$ is fitted with a quadratic and $-3.03\%$ (s.d.\ $0.31\%$) with a cubic (ddof $=1$; the pairs come from three geometries and are not independent). The deficit has a mild wavelength trend (about $-2.4\%$ at \SI{1520}{nm} to $-3.2\%$ at \SI{1567}{nm}). We do not use the solver's reported $n_g$ column as the reference because it is not smooth in wavelength (below); it gives $-2.73\%$ with s.d.\ $0.72\%$.

Four candidate explanations are excluded by measurement. \emph{Round-trip
length:} the straight fraction $2L_c/L$ is $3.5\%$, $5.8\%$ and $10.9\%$ for
the three geometries --- a factor of three --- and the deficit is identical
for all three, whereas a length error or a straight/bend mixing error would
scale with it. \emph{Bend effects:} $0.08\%$, above. \emph{FSR extraction:}
an independent re-extraction from the saved spectra agrees with the
production analysis to $0.03\%$, $0.005\%$ and $0.014\%$. \emph{The $n_g$
polynomial:} $0.26\%$ at these geometries. What remains was traced to the FDTD material model. FDTD represents each database material by a fit over the source band, and for silicon (Palik) over \SIrange{1500}{1600}{nm} that fit has \emph{no} dispersion: its group index equals its phase index ($3.4766$ for both), against $3.642$ for the raw data, and the same holds for silica ($1.4440$). We tested the consequence on a plain straight \SI{450}{nm}$\times$\SI{220}{nm} guide, with no ring and no bend, in 3-D FDTD at four mesh sizes. The mode-solver group index is $4.329$. FDTD gives $4.219$, $4.221$, $4.182$ and $4.174$ at 40, 32, 25 and \SI{20}{nm}, which is $-2.5$ to $-3.6\%$, the size of the ring's deficit, and the gap widens as the mesh is refined, so it is not numerical dispersion. Replacing both materials by fixed indices changes the FDTD result by less than $2\times10^{-5}$, confirming that the engine already behaves as if the materials were dispersionless, while the mode solver with the same fixed indices gives $4.147$; the FDTD values lie $+1.8$, $+0.9$ and $+0.7\%$ above that at 40, 25 and \SI{20}{nm}, converging toward it. The difference of $0.18$ between the dispersive and fixed-index solver values is the material contribution to the guide's group index, which the FDTD engine omits. We then gave FDTD a dispersive fit instead (the sampled Palik index entered as permittivity, whose fitted group index is $3.626$ for silicon against $3.642$ raw and $1.4638$ for silica against $1.4639$) and repeated the straight-guide runs. The group index becomes $4.390$, $4.349$ and $4.341$ at 40, 25 and \SI{20}{nm}, i.e.\ $+1.4$, $+0.5$ and $+0.3\%$ against the mode solver, a small positive error that shrinks with the mesh, in place of $-2.5$, $-3.4$ and $-3.6\%$ with the default fit. Restoring the dispersion therefore removes the gap. We attribute the ring's deficit to this omission and not to the coupled-mode assembly. Two limits apply. The ring itself was not re-simulated with a dispersive silicon model, so the cause is demonstrated for the straight guide and inferred for the ring from the agreement in size. And the $\kappa^2$ values from coupler FDTD used the same material fit; we expect the effect to be small at \SI{1550}{nm}, where the index is correct, but did not measure it: an attempt with the dispersive fit was numerically unstable in the long 3-D coupler runs (field energy grew with time and the transmitted power exceeded the input), so those runs were discarded. The mode-solver $n_g$ agrees with the measured device of [11] ($4.344$ against $4.345$ implied), so the dataset labels, which use the mode solver, are not affected.

Two caveats attach to the sweep itself. The FDE solver intermittently returns
a degenerate result from an identical call sequence, so each solve is
verified and repeated until a guided mode is found. And the solver's reported
$n_g$ is not smooth in wavelength: successive steps for the straight
\SI{450}{nm} guide are $+0.0085$, $+0.0878$, $+0.0031$, $-0.0250$, one of
them an order of magnitude larger than its neighbours. Against a
finite-difference $n_g$ computed from the solver's own $n_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)$
the reported values scatter with a standard deviation of $0.54\%$. We
therefore do \emph{not} regenerate the dataset labels from these values: the
correction they would apply ($0.26\%$) is smaller than the noise they would
inject.

\textbf{The validation itself was mesh-limited, and at low to moderate $\QL$
the analytic model is correct to within 2\%.} Simulated $\QL$ depends on the
mesh, so the \SI{32}{nm} spacing --- established as converged for $\kappa^2$,
a single-pass quantity --- is not converged for $\QL$, which accumulates loss
over hundreds of round trips. For the two lower-$\QL$ geometries, refinement
moves the simulation \emph{toward} the analytic prediction and a second-order
extrapolation to zero mesh recovers it to $1.2\%$ and $1.9\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:ringval}). A discrepancy of 4--6\% at the production mesh is
therefore attributed to numerical error for these two geometries; with only two geometries and two mesh sizes this is an inference, not a convergence proof.

\textbf{At the highest $\QL$ the discrepancy is real and unexplained.} For the
third geometry, refinement moves the simulation \emph{away} from the model.
The tripled spectral sampling changed $\QL$ by $0.45\%$, so this is neither an
extraction nor a sampling artifact. One candidate is that the bend-loss model
is a function of radius alone and carries no wavelength dependence, while this
geometry resonates at \SI{1555}{nm} against \SI{1542}{nm} and \SI{1543}{nm}
for the other two; bend radiation at this radius increases steeply toward
longer wavelengths, so a wavelength-blind loss model would over-predict $\QL$
for the reddest resonance, and a finer mesh resolving genuine radiation would
lower it. We state this as a hypothesis: testing it requires a
wavelength-resolved bend-loss study that we have not performed.

\textbf{Insertion loss does not validate.} Drop-port peak transmission differs
from the analytic prediction by $-12.5\%$, $+1.5\%$ and $-17.9\%$ at the
production mesh, with no consistent sign. One of the three is not evidence at
all: that geometry's IL is \SI{0.084}{dB}, i.e.\ $T = 0.981$, within 2\% of
unity, where the absolute uncertainty of extracting IL from a simulated
spectrum is comparable to the value itself.

\textbf{What this does and does not affect.} Every model compared in
the Results was trained and scored on identical labels, so a bias shared by all
of them cancels from every model-to-model comparison: the assessment of O6,
the metric result of Section~\ref{sec:metric}, the realisability result and
the sample-efficiency comparison are unaffected. What must be qualified is the
absolute accuracy of $\QL$ above $\QL \approx 4300$, the absolute accuracy of
IL anywhere, and the scenario verdicts, which are judged against $\QL$ specifications. Of the 1{,}150 labels, $84.7\%$ have $\QL$ above 4300 and $76.3\%$ above 6351 (the analytic $\QL$ of the highest-$\QL$ geometry simulated), so most absolute $\QL$ and IL labels lie outside the range this validation supports.
